## Working inside the loop, in houses built across a century

River Oaks was laid out in the 1920s, and the housing stock still reflects that. Streets near River Oaks Boulevard, Homewood and Country Club Estates hold original houses from the 1920s through the 1940s, many of them carefully maintained and several times renovated. Other lots carry replacement construction from the last two decades. Two houses on the same block can need completely different work.

## Ceilings and plaster in older River Oaks homes

Ceiling work is the most common first call in this part of Houston, and it is also where the age of the house matters most. Pre-war homes here frequently have plaster over wood lath rather than gypsum board. Plaster cracks along its own lines, keys break away from the lath above, and a patch made with a modern drywall approach can leave a visible ridge where the two materials meet.

Crown molding, cove transitions and decorative plaster detail raise the difficulty again, because the repair has to return to a profile rather than a flat plane. Tall ceilings mean access and staging before any finishing starts, and long window walls throw raking light across the surface that will find an unconvincing blend. None of that is a reason to avoid the repair. It is a reason to look at the ceiling in person rather than quote it from a photograph.

Newer construction in River Oaks has modern board and machine-applied texture, which blends more predictably. The honest answer about how invisible a repair will be depends on which of those two houses we are standing in.

### Water damage and what causes it here

Leaks in this area come from the same sources as the rest of Houston: roofs after storms, air-conditioning condensate drains, supply lines after a freeze, and second-floor bathrooms. Older homes add their own paths, including original galvanized plumbing that has been partially replaced over the years and flat or low-slope roof sections on additions.

Parts of River Oaks sit close to Buffalo Bayou, and the neighborhood has seen significant flood events. Interior repair after water intrusion should follow drying, not race it. When material is wet, the affected area is opened, the wet board or plaster is removed, the cavity dries, and only then does new material go back. Closing a damp cavity is faster on the first day and expensive afterward.

When an insurance claim may be involved, document the damage before demolition. OilerBilt does not act as an adjuster and does not promise claim coverage; the scope should match the condition actually found once safe access is available.

## Deed restrictions, permits and access

Houston has no zoning, but that does not mean River Oaks has no rules. The neighborhood is governed by deed restrictions administered by its property owners organization, and those architectural controls are entirely separate from City of Houston permitting. Exterior changes in particular can require review that a permit search will never show.

City of Houston permits still apply to work that changes plumbing, electrical systems or structural framing. Interior finish repair generally does not, but the address and the real scope decide that, so we confirm both before answering.

Practical access matters here as well. Narrow driveways, mature trees, gated entries and limited street parking affect staging, material delivery and debris removal. Those are worked out during the site visit rather than discovered on the first morning.
